MythTrace: a health AI that can't be fed myths

An AI health assistant is only as trustworthy as the pages it reads. We built one, tried to poison it with fake pages based on real Indian health myths, and defended it with search techniques we wrote from scratch.

the problem

Whoever ranks first decides the answer

Many AI assistants use retrieval-augmented generation (RAG): they first search for a few relevant passages, then ask the AI to answer using only those passages, with citations. This reduces made-up answers, but it moves the trust problem to search. If someone can get their page ranked high, they decide what the AI says.

The PoisonedRAG paper showed that adding just five fake texts per question to a database of millions was enough to fool a system 90% of the time. The fake text is written to match the question closely, which is exactly what a ranking function rewards.

In India, health myths spread fast. If an assistant retrieves "bitter gourd juice cures diabetes, so stop your medicine" and repeats it, someone could stop their treatment.

Our question: can classic search techniques, built from scratch, keep planted myths away from the AI without making normal search worse?

what we built

Every search step, built from scratch

No library does the indexing or ranking. We crawled 1,000 MedlinePlus pages politely (following robots.txt, one request per second), split them into 2,762 sections, and built our own inverted index, tf-idf and BM25 ranking, and PageRank over the link graph. The top five sections go to Claude, which must cite a source after every claim and say "I don't have enough information" when the sources don't cover the question.

Then we attacked it

Antara wrote 16 fake pages from real myths (bitter gourd, papaya leaf, fenugreek, giloy, amla). Half sat on fake websites with no links pointing to them. The other half were hidden inside real MedlinePlus pages, as if a trusted site had been hacked.

And defended it

Each section's final score is its relevance, plus a trust bonus from PageRank and trusted sites, minus a penalty for each red flag:

score = relevance + 0.3 × trust − 0.5 × flags

The flags come from two cheap, explainable checks: does the page copy the question word for word, and does it repeat keywords unnaturally? We set the thresholds using 2 fake pages, then froze them and tested on the other 14, so we couldn't tune our way to good numbers.

The AI is a useful last line of defense, but we didn't want to rely on it alone. A weaker model or a subtler myth might not be caught. And even when the AI rejects a myth, the fake page still takes one of only five source slots, pushing real information out. Measuring that "crowding out" was something checking answers alone would have missed.

All five questions we deliberately left unanswerable (prices, a government scheme, a local doctor, live case counts) got "I don't have enough information", with no invented details.

limitations

What still gets through

  • Subtle hidden fakes. 5 of 8 fake sections hidden in real pages still reached the top five. A lightly stuffed section on a trusted page looks statistically like a genuine one.
  • Cross-question leaks. The copy check only catches pages stuffed with the current question's words. The fenugreek page still ranked 3rd for the bitter gourd question.
  • Word matching failures. For "warning signs of a heart attack", the words "attack" and "warning" pulled in panic attack pages, so the AI correctly declined to answer.
  • Small evaluation. 16 fake pages and 19 scored questions, English MedlinePlus only.

Next

Boost results where query words appear close together (using the positional index we already have), expand queries to match word forms like treat and treatment, and flag a section that contradicts the rest of its own page.
